01 · Origin

The god who was caught
stealing flowers.

Indra, god of rain and heaven, came down to Kathmandu disguised as an ordinary man, searching for pārijāta flowers for his mother. The people did not recognise him. They caught him as a common thief and locked him away.

His mother descended in search of her missing son. When he was released, she made a promise to the valley in return — dew and mist, enough to carry the harvest through the year. That single exchange, a captured god and a mother's bargain, is the seed from which every part of Indra Jatra grows.

02

Yosin — the pole that opens the festival

A sacred wooden pole raised at Basantapur, before Hanuman Dhoka, at the very start of Indra Jatra. It carries Indra's flag over the city for the length of the festival. When it comes down, the festival ends with it.

03 · The Search

Dāgin

A mother, still looking for her son

Dāgin represents Indra's mother, searching the city for the boy the crowd mistook for a thief. Her procession moves through Kathmandu's old streets, re-enacting that search exactly as the legend describes it.

She is not a myth kept in a temple. Every year, she walks again — the oldest grief in the festival, given a body.

04 · The Elephant

Pulukisi

Airāvata, Indra's own elephant

While Dāgin searches as a mother, Pulukisi searches as a companion — Indra's elephant Airāvata, turned loose through the city to find its captured master.

The procession is famously unruly: the elephant charges, sways, and barrels through crowds, exactly as an animal that has lost its master would. Protection, in this festival, often looks like chaos first.

05 · The Protector

Lakhe

Majipa Lakhe — fearsome, and devoted to it

Lakhe descends from an old Newar tradition of supernatural, masked dance — long before it belonged to Indra Jatra at all. Legend holds that a powerful Lakhe became bound to the Majipa neighbourhood and was allowed to remain in the city, on one condition: that it protect its children.

So Lakhe is frightening by design, and a guardian by choice. Fear and protection, worn as the same mask.

Jhyalincha — the mischievous child who teases Lakhe and darts away, daring the mask to give chase. Their play is not a sideshow; it is the community's own relationship with something powerful, acted out in front of it.

06 · The Newar Layer

Faces without photographs, still central to the story.

Not every figure in Indra Jatra appears as a mask you can see up close. Some are worshipped in place, glimpsed once a year, or carried only in a chariot.

Bhairav

The fierce form of Shiva

A powerful protective deity central to Newar religious life. During Indra Jatra, forms such as Sweta Bhairav and Akash Bhairav are unveiled and worshipped, and Bhairav rides again in the later chariot procession.

Ganesh

Auspiciousness, riding alongside

Ganesh belongs mainly to the later Kumari-chariot tradition rather than the original Indra story. He accompanies Kumari and Bhairav, clearing the way and marking the procession as auspicious.

Sweta & Akash Bhairav

Displayed, not paraded

Large ceremonial faces of Bhairav are opened to public view only during Indra Jatra — a rare, once-a-year audience with a form usually kept behind closed doors.

07 · The Living Goddess

Kumari

A child, and a goddess, at once

The story goes that King Jaya Prakash Malla once met the goddess Taleju in secret to play dice. When his daughter discovered them, Taleju vanished — then returned in a dream, telling the king to find her a young Shakya girl to inhabit. Some chronicles credit an earlier king, Trailokya Malla, with formalising the institution itself.

Her chariot joined Indra Jatra only in the 18th century, under Jaya Prakash Malla — centuries after Dāgin and Pulukisi first walked these same streets. The newest tradition, now the one most visitors come to see.

08 · The Royal Layer

Three chariots, one procession.

Added centuries after Indra's own story, the three-chariot procession is the festival's youngest tradition — and its most public face today.

Divine Power

Kumari

The Living Goddess herself, carried through the old city — divine female power made visible.

Auspiciousness

Ganesh

Rides ahead, removing obstacles and marking the path as favourable.

Protection

Bhairav

Closes the procession with the fierce guardianship the city has relied on for centuries.

09 · The Rhythm

Dhimey Beat

Underneath every procession, the same drum. Dhimey is the heartbeat of Newar festivals — its rhythm doesn't just accompany Indra Jatra, it moves the whole city through it, from the raising of the Yosin to the last chariot home.

In Short

One festival, three centuries deep.

Indra Jatra didn't arrive whole. It grew in layers, and every layer is still visible if you know where to look.

Oldest Core

Indra, rain, and the harvest

The Yosin pole, Dāgin's search, and Pulukisi's procession — the original story of a captured god, a mother's bargain, and the dew that feeds the fields.

Newar Layer

Lakhe, Jhyalincha, and Bhairav

Older, independent Newar traditions of masked protection and fierce deity worship, woven so deeply into the festival that they now feel original to it.

Royal Layer

Kumari, Ganesh, and the three chariots

Added under the Malla kings, centuries after the rest — the Living Goddess procession that most visitors now know Indra Jatra by.

Ancient Indra traditions, Newar religious life, and later royal ceremony — Indra Jatra is what happens when three histories agree to share one week in the same streets.
